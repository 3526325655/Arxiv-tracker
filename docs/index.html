Fine-tuning Multi-modal LLMs with ART: Art-based Reinforcement Training
Authors: Michal Chudoba, Sergey Alyaev, Petra Galuscakova, Tomasz Wiktorski
First: 2026-06-10T09:30:37+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-29T17:56:43+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Adapting Large Language Models (LLMs) to specific tasks usually involves fine-tuning: Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) edits the weights, while soft prompting prepends trainable virtual tokens. Both change how an already-optimized model is served, so serving many task-specific adapters at high throughput requires adapter-aware infrastructure. We propose Art-Based Reinforcement Training (ART), which optimizes a single auxiliary input image to specialize a frozen Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) for a specific task. At inference time, ART simply includes this image in each standard multimodal prompt, requiring no changes to the serving engine. We compare ART with LoRA and soft prompting across five reasoning benchmarks for four small MLLMs from three architectures. While different methods perform best for different tasks, ART ranks first or second in 10 of 20 benchmark-MLLM pairs, with its best results on the two math word problem benchmarks (GSM8k and SVAMP). It is significantly ahead of the next best adapter in 2 cases, within the noise of the next best adapter in 6, and significantly above the text-only baseline in 18. ART therefore reaches accuracy close to LoRA and soft prompts while serving faster than LoRA. In the vLLM serving stack, ART reduces throughput by 6% to 14% relative to text-only requests, compared to LoRA's 13% to 93% slowdown. Beyond efficient serving, ART artifacts are plain images, making them easy to store, distribute, and share. This approach keep task-specific adaptation outside the model weights, so the same frozen model can support different tasks without separate model variants.

TextSeal2: Distortion-Free LLM Watermarking with Copula Mixing and Memoryless Races
Authors: Tom Sander, Pierre Fernandez, Itai Gat, Haiming Bao, Jiaxin Shi, Jason Park, Valeriu Lacatusu, Sylvestre-Alvise Rebuffi, Hongyan Chang, Hady Elsahar, Tomáš Souček, Tuan Tran, Surya Parimi, Alexandre Mourachko

Comments: Code: https://github.com/facebookresearch/textseal
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
We present TextSeal2, a distortion-free watermark for large language models that keeps its signal under speculative decoding: tokens that replace rejected drafts remain watermarked with the same keys, and the acceptance rate is unchanged. It also preserves output diversity and adds no measurable inference latency. Its predecessor, TextSeal, restores diversity in the Gumbel-max scheme by routing each token to one of two keys via a coin flip. This is suboptimal because the detector averages the scores from both keys without knowing which key was used. TextSeal2 instead merges two or more keys into a single virtual key and couples it to fresh noise through AutoMark's Gaussian copula. Any number of keys, including publicly shared keys, can be merged this way without diluting detection. Under speculative decoding, TextSeal2 verifies keyed drafts with one more exponential race, run on the leftovers of the draft races. By memorylessness, this race reproduces block verification, so the output follows the target model exactly. We evaluate TextSeal2 on three LLMs. At equal output token diversity, TextSeal2 detects 8 to 22 percentage points more 100-token answers than TextSeal at a false-positive rate of $10^{-3}$, and is comparable to textGrain while requiring no post-logits optimization. Under speculative decoding, it detects 18 and 22 percentage points more 200-token answers than TextSeal and textGrain, respectively, at the same acceptance rate.

Abstract
An autonomous research laboratory could conduct an entire study, from formulating questions to preparing manuscripts. Artificial intelligence (AI) offers one route towards this goal. Recent AI systems take on several research roles, but may draw conclusions that the evidence does not support. Here, we present an autonomous research laboratory in which agents based on large language models assess one another's work and can stop or redirect investigations. They can generate and reject research questions, require separate theoretical checks and conduct experiments designed to challenge their claims. We demonstrate these capabilities by investigating ensembles of quantum spins in diamond. The agents derived the minimum number of calibration settings needed to predict responses to a defined family of microwave pulse sequences. They checked the result computationally and designed experiments to distinguish competing predictions. An initial experiment agreed with the theoretical predictions, but repeated reference measurements showed that the signal was drifting during data collection. The agents therefore treated the result as inconclusive and revised the measurement protocol without relaxing the acceptance criteria. The revised experiment met those criteria and supported the theoretical predictions. This study demonstrates that collaborating AI agents can select research questions, derive theoretical results and test their predictions experimentally.

Abstract
This study investigates the capabilities of Large Language Models to simulate counselling clients in educational role-plays in comparison to human role-players. Initially, we recorded role-playing sessions, where novice counsellors interacted with human peers acting as clients, followed by role-plays between humans and clients simulated by Mistrals Mixtral 8x7b using 4-bit quantization. Abstract
Clinical genomic analysis requires experts to integrate evolving guidelines, databases, and computational evidence. As sequencing has become increasingly accessible, this complex and labor-intensive process has become a major bottleneck to the timely use of genomic information in clinical decision-making. To fill this gap, we developed ClinGeX, a large-language-model agent that composes 105 curated executable skills through task-specific retrieval and dependency-aware orchestration. These findings support executable domain skills as a promising approach to augment specialist genomic interpretation.

On the Fundamental Limits of Prefill-Based Decoding Length Prediction
Authors: Haowen Shi, Xuzhe Yan, Fahao Chen, Peng Li
First: 2026-10-29T17:36:01+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-29T17:36:01+00:00
Comments: 26 pages, 4 figures
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Predicting how many tokens a large language model (LLM) will decode, using only prefill-phase hidden states, is a key primitive for inference scheduling. We show that current benchmarks conflate predictor quality with task hardness: when the target model, dataset, or sampling configuration changes, reported accuracy collapses even though the predictor is unchanged. We trace this instability to an overlooked error source---the inherent stochasticity of autoregressive decoding---and derive bounds that every prefill-side method must obey. Our analysis treats decoding as a stopped stochastic process. The per-step stopping probability of a response is not constant but ramps up over hundreds of steps, so the decode length inherits a central limit theorem from the many local decisions that determine where a response ends; this yields per-prompt normality with an explicit error term, together with an affine variance law $σ=αμ+b$ whose slope is the coefficient of variation of the number of response segments. Empirically, the normal law reproduces the per-prompt MAE and the central mass of the decode-length distribution to within a few percent, and the residual departures from normality (mild right skew, slight excess kurtosis) are the ones predicted by the sampling-noise correction. Under fixed model, data, and sampling conditions, a broad design space of predictors (MLP, EGTP, FiLM, soft-label, and Mixture-of-Experts variants) converges to similar performance close to the bound, confirming that \emph{system error dominates model error}. We argue for a benchmarking protocol that fixes the model, data, and sampling parameters and that reports relative-error metrics alongside absolute ones. Code and analysis tools are available at https://github.com/ShwStone/DecodingPredict.

PhysCount: Do MLLMs Count Visual Appearances or Physical Entities?
Authors: Lidong Lu, Jiaxin Lv, Liangrui Dong, Xinyi Mao, Lidong Zou, Zhenquan Li, Tong Lu, Guo Chen
First: 2026-10-29T17:33:34+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-29T17:33:34+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Images reveal appearances, but counting often asks about physical objects. A single entity may be visible directly, in several reflections, or through glass, so detecting every visible occurrence does not determine how many entities are present. Existing visual counting benchmarks rarely separate these two operations. We introduce PhysCount, a benchmark for evaluating whether multimodal large language models (MLLMs) can distinguish what appears in an image from what physically exists in the scene. PhysCount contains 546 real-world images and 4,595 human-written questions organized into 651 target-specific groups. For each group, annotators locate the relevant appearances, label how each is observed, and identify appearances of the same entity. Four complementary question families test the enumeration of appearances, the enumeration of physical entities, image-formation-specific counting, and appearance--entity correspondence. We measure both accuracy on each family and the proportion of groups answered entirely correctly. Across 40 MLLMs, counting visible appearances is substantially easier than recovering the identities that connect them. Even the strongest model answers every question correctly in only 13.36\% of groups. Performance declines when one entity produces many appearances or when its appearances cross observation modes. Higher image resolution improves access to visual evidence but brings limited gains in complete-group accuracy, whereas answering related questions jointly produces a marked improvement. These results expose a gap between recognizing what is visible and correctly recovering the physical entities behind those observations.

Images reveal appearances, but counting often asks about physical objects.
VoiceLongMemEval: Do Assistants Remember How You Sounded?

Abstract
With the growing scale of multi-agent architectures and large language models, deployed AI assistants are increasingly tasked with reasoning over long, continuous, multi-session conversation histories. Current benchmarks evaluate this dialogue history as information retrieval over long horizon, temporal reasoning, or knowledge updates, while crucially ignoring the fundamental dynamics of human-agent interaction, i.e. how they said it. To address this gap, we present VoiceLongMemEval (VLME) benchmark, where every answer depends on paralinguistic metadata (emotion labels, prosody descriptors, and voice events) attached to conversational turns, which is otherwise unrecoverable from the words alone. Every item passes a three-stage adversarial gate, ensuring that a strong language model fails when given only the transcript. Evaluating leading frontier and open-weight models reveals a pervasive affect gap; providing text-track paralinguistic metadata yields a 0.09 to 0.38 accuracy boost (0.61 to 0.69 when prompted with evidence hints), while standard ASR pipelines systematically discard this signal. Additionally, audio-native models successfully extract these cues directly from speech (0.354 to 0.412 vs. 0.325 blind). Code and dataset will be made available upon acceptance.

How I Aligned Your Model: Scaling Up Multilingual Safety Training with Templates

Abstract
Safety alignment for Large Language Models is heavily focused on English with limited multilingual representation, particularly damaging low-resource language performance. Related open training datasets are often limited in size or do not follow region-localized harmful topics to broaden the scope of alignment beyond cultures. We propose a scalable template-based augmentation pipeline to produce massive multilingual refusal and benign corpora, extracting safe templates from harmful prompts, translating them and then filling in the slots with culturally authentic entities, both harmful and benign. As a result, we release MSafeTemp, a validated corpus spanning 60 languages useful for multilingual safety alignment and evaluation. Using the dataset, we show that English-only evaluation misjudges multilingual safety: for several models, attacks that fail in English succeed once translated, while culturally localized variants expose further failures that translation alone misses. We also confirm that augmenting safety training data through templates allows for higher diversity and quality of alignment, resulting in improved safety performance beyond English. MSafeTemp and the respective pipeline are aimed at facilitating research and development of safer and more responsible models for less-represented cultures.

Abstract
Context: Generative AI (GenAI) and Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used for academic tasks in software engineering and beyond, including systematic literature reviews (SLRs). However, whilst their capabilities are rapidly developing, there is no guarantee they can meet the rigour, reliability, and transparency that SLRs require. By standardizing how these tools are deployed and evaluated, the GUEST framework provides a necessary methodological baseline for trustworthy GenAI-assisted research in software engineering.

Abstract
Large language model (LLM) agents for personal health read wearable data and recommend, but do not learn about the individual in a way that can be audited or falsified. We present EpiCoach, an agentic system that treats one athlete, a cross-country mountain biker, as the subject of accountable N-of-1 science. Claims about the athlete live in an evidence-gated hypothesis registry: each must issue a probabilistic prediction about a future session before it occurs, and its belief moves only through computed evidence from a logged statistical fit. Predictions come from a mechanistic digital twin inferred from heart rate, GPS and barometric altitude: a lagged heart-rate response model, a fitness-fatigue model with learned time constants, and a latent technical-descending skill state, measured on discovered trail segments. The agent prescribes the next training week as a constrained experiment maximizing information gain subject to load and safety constraints, around two instrument rides. We evaluate the system two ways over eighteen months of data. A rolling-origin replay benchmark shows the twin beats standard forecasters on heart rate but not segment time, and the agentic loop matches a static model. A live prospective phase runs the weekly cycle: in its first three weeks the ledger sealed 20 predictions, scored 14, the descent forecast calibrated after one correction, the climb forecast pessimistic as the athlete improved, and the safety officer vetoed the agent twice. Every correction is preserved in the registry, a template for auditable, falsifiable personalization in digital health. We release the registry, benchmark and protocol.

A Unified Hessian-Guided Framework for Post-Training Quantization
Authors: Mads Toftrup, Marco Federici, Boris van Breugel, Paul Whatmough, Markus Nagel
First: 2026-10-29T16:06:01+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-29T16:06:01+00:00
Comments: 10 pages + 4 appendix
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Low-bit quantization enables efficient deployment of large language models by reducing memory footprint and computational cost. Achieving high accuracy at low precision requires selecting quantization ranges, linear transformations, and weight rounding decisions. Existing approaches typically optimize these components independently using local reconstruction objectives, which fail to account for the varying impact of quantization errors on downstream predictions. Direct optimization of task-level metrics would provide a more faithful objective, but is challenging due to the discontinuous nature of quantization operations and the high computational cost of evaluating their downstream effects. In this work, we derive a Hessian-guided approximation of the output change induced by joint weight and activation quantization. Based on a tractable Fisher approximation, we formulate a unified post-training quantization framework that enables the joint optimization of quantization ranges, linear transformations, and weight rounding under a common output-sensitivity objective. The resulting method remains practical for modern large language models and consistently outperforms state-of-the-art transformation and rounding methods across both channel-wise and block-wise quantization settings.

Abstract
Radiological risk stratification and management planning are essential downstream tasks in medical imaging. Automating these tasks is challenging because clinical guideline criteria are distributed across prose, tables, and cross-referenced definitions. Abstract
Prefill-decode (PD) disaggregation isolates the two phases of large language model serving, but request-level path selection must account for prefill work, cache reuse, and decode demand. Only Accepted Tokens Count: An Energy Accounting of Speculative Decoding Under Latency Targets
Authors: Bole Ma, Ayesha Afzal, Jan Eitzinger, Harald Koestler, Gerhard Wellein
First: 2026-10-29T15:37:45+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-29T15:37:45+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large language model serving is limited by facility power. Energy per token determines how many tokens a datacenter can serve, while a latency target bounds how quickly each user receives them. During decode, every weight is read from memory for every generated token. Batching amortizes this read across users but makes no individual user faster. Speculative decoding does: a drafter proposes several future tokens, and the target model verifies them in a single forward pass that reuses the same weight read. Because every drafted token must be verified whether or not it is accepted, the energy speculation saves depends on how many drafted tokens are accepted. This saving is hard to pin down. It varies across target models from different attention families and across drafting schemes, and it depends on the text being served. Every drafter we evaluate accepts fewer tokens on conversation than on code, and every trained drafter accepts fewer on Romansh, a low-resource language, than on English. We measure speculative decoding under production serving conditions (SGLang, CUDA graphs enabled) on H100 and H200 GPUs, at batch sizes up to 64 across 5 corpora. On dense models, verification, not drafting, dominates the energy cost. The saving shrinks as the batch grows, because batching has already shared the weight read that speculation would divide among the accepted tokens. At large batch sizes, block drafters push verification past the balance point between arithmetic and memory traffic into the compute-bound regime, where the saving becomes a loss, and a drafter with low acceptance raises energy per token. Meeting a tighter latency target with speculation then costs additional energy. Larger batches lower energy per token without speculation. Speculation adds to that saving only when acceptance is high, and on the model we tested it leaves little headroom for latency-preserving DVFS.
Summary / 总结
Large language model serving is limited by facility power.
Neighborhood Elites: Quality-Diversity Search for Uncertainty Quantification in Question Answering
Authors: Mesut Ceylan, Arda Baris Basaran, Elgar Fleisch, Filipe Barata
First: 2026-10-29T15:32:57+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-29T15:32:57+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly capable, yet they produce wrong answers indistinguishable from correct ones. In question answering (QA), such an error propagates unnoticed into downstream tasks. Perturbation-based uncertainty quantification (UQ) addresses this by probing a model over a set of input rewrites and reading uncertainty from how much its predictions vary. Yet this set is usually built generically, by free paraphrasing, token-level perturbation, or adversarial search. Such rewrites leave two things uncontrolled that matter in QA: what kind of change is made to the context, and how explicitly the context supports the answer. When neither is controlled, the composition of the set is determined by the generator rather than by design, and the estimate inherits whatever that generator happens to produce. Quality-diversity (QD) search is built to cover a predefined exploration space, so we recast input perturbation as a QD problem and propose \emph{neighborhood elites (NE)}, a neighborhood constructor that spreads answer-preserving rewrites across two axes, the kind of edit and the explicitness of support. We compare NE against three other neighborhood constructors, holding the editor, QA model, uncertainty measure and search objective fixed, and against three scores read from the original context. Across five extractive-QA datasets and five instruction-tuned LLMs, NE improves failure detection against every other constructor in at least 24 of the 25 dataset-model pairs, reaching 0.726 AUROC and 0.211 AUPRC lift over the base rate. Additionally, NE detects failure better than semantic entropy computed over decoder samples, by 0.074 AUROC and 0.062 AUPRC lift, and this advantage widens as the neighborhood grows.

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly capable, yet they produce wrong answers indistinguishable from correct ones.

Abstract
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) power platforms like ChatGPT, Gemini, and Copilot, enabling rich interactions across text, images, and videos. Unlike text-only workloads, multimodal requests are inherently heterogeneous, differing by orders of magnitude in latency and memory footprint. Existing LLM serving systems, designed for homogeneous text workloads, struggle under this heterogeneity: large image and video requests monopolize GPU resources, causing severe head-of-line blocking and degrading the responsiveness of lightweight, latency-critical text queries. Recent multimodal serving systems mitigate this interference through disaggregation, isolating encoding and inference stages across GPUs. While effective at large scale, such designs introduce severe GPU underutilization in more practical, moderate-scale deployments. In response, we build RPS-Serve a modality-aware scheduling framework that leverages the extreme heterogeneity in multimodal workloads and introduces a simple abstraction: videos behave like rocks, images like pebbles, and text like sand. Then, it lets sand flow quickly through pebbles and rocks, maximizing GPU utilization while ensuring fast responsiveness for lightweight requests and controlled execution for heavier ones. To achieve this, RPS-Serve estimates the GPU admission cost of each request, classifies requests into rocks, pebbles, and sand using lightweight clustering, and applies priority-based scheduling with aging to balance performance across modalities. Evaluation across state-of-the-art MLLMs shows that RPS-Serve increases GPU utilization by 65% and reduces TTFT by up to 9.3x, on average across models, compared to disaggregation-based multi-modal systems.

Abstract
Computer-use agents built on large language models (LLMs) work in software that can change while they act: an appointment moves, a policy is revised, a record is updated. First: 2026-10-14T00:37:23+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-29T15:10:37+00:00

Abstract
AI agents use a large language model (LLM) to plan and act over many steps. Which model should an agent use, so that its tasks are completed reliably with the least energy, cost and time? We treat this as a multi-criteria decision problem in which comparisons per call can mislead: a task succeeds only if all its steps succeed, and failed attempts still use resources. We therefore compare models on resources per successfully completed task. We derive crossover task lengths at which two models exchange their efficiency ranking, determined by their per-step reliability and resource use. Each objective has its own crossover. Below the shortest crossover the less reliable model dominates, above the longest the more reliable one dominates, and in between both are Pareto-efficient and preferences decide. This supports a two-stage framework: a preference-free dominance screen, followed by a multi-criteria ranking of the efficient models, subject to reliability and feasibility constraints. We also give conditions under which this structure holds when reliability and resource use change during a task. In a pilot with 15 open-weight models on two synthetic agent tasks, with measured GPU energy, the predicted crossover fell within the observed range for 72 of the 88 observed crossovers. At 20 steps, a 3-billion-parameter model used a tenth of the energy of a 27-billion one per completed task; at 100 steps, a 1.7-billion model used almost four times as much as an 8-billion one. Smaller is greener only up to a task length that can be predicted.

Abstract
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) can now process hour-long videos, making extended multi-turn dialogue over a single video a natural mode of use. Sustaining such a dialogue requires more than answering each question in isolation: the model must retrieve and reuse information established earlier in the conversation, both what it has seen and what it has said, an ability we term in-context video memory. Existing video QA benchmarks leave this ability unmeasured: their questions are mutually independent, never reference one another, and each is answerable from the video alone, so nothing ever requires carrying information across turns. We introduce VideoMemBench, in which each sample pairs a single video with a long multi-turn dialogue, whose turns are connected by typed cross-turn dependencies: an Episodic Dependency refers back to a visual episode described in an earlier turn, probing memory of what the model has seen, while an Answer Dependency builds on the model's own earlier answer, probing memory of what the model has said. Every dependency is verified considering two properties: 1) necessity, meaning the question cannot be answered without its earlier turns, and 2) non-leakage, meaning the question does not restate the information from its earlier turns. To enforce these properties, we design a construction pipeline with three LLM roles: a planner, a generator, and a judger, with each dialogue reviewed by human experts. VideoMemBench comprises 326 videos with 2,476 turns, of which 1,387 depend on earlier turns, including multi-parent dependencies and long-range call-back. Among over 20 evaluated open- and closed-source MLLMs, Claude Opus 5 performs the best with an overall score of only 45.7\%, highlighting the challenge of in-context video memory. Our dataset and evaluation are released.

Abstract
Fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) can induce behaviors beyond those explicitly targeted. For example, training a model to respond more warmly can make it more sycophantic, and training it to write insecure code can cause broad misalignment on prompts unrelated to coding. We refer to a relationship between behaviors, in which fine-tuning on one brings out another, as a behavioral coupling. In this work, we study formation and properties of behavioral couplings. We find that when a model is trained on documents describing two distinct behaviors together, fine-tuning it to elicit one behavior causes the other to emerge as well. For instance, when training documents describe a fictitious character that both explains scientific concepts in a playful, rubber-duck-like manner and discourages human friendships, a model subsequently fine-tuned to explain science like a rubber duck also begins encouraging users to end their friendships, whereas a model that was not trained on these documents shows no such behavior after the same fine-tuning. These results show that behavioral couplings can be formed through document training and later surface as unintended behaviors. More concerningly, we find that couplings can compose. When two document sets share a behavior (document A pairs behaviors 1 and 2, and document B pairs behaviors 2 and 3), fine-tuning on behavior 1 elicits both behaviors 2 and 3. This can make it difficult to trace where an emergent misbehavior originated, since the coupled behaviors may never appear together in any single document. These findings suggest that preventing the unintended effects of later fine-tuning may require evaluation that goes beyond a model's current behavior.

Abstract
Effective multi-domain adaptation of large language models requires prioritizing relevant domain knowledge while minimizing interference from irrelevant domains. Existing approaches commonly use Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to train a separate adapter for each domain and combine these adapters within one model. However, they either weight adapters equally regardless of the input domain or dynamically weight adapters while still activating all of them including irrelevant ones. Additionally, while efficient, their low-rank structure limits performance. With just 256 examples per domain for router supervision, DS-Upcycling achieves 90-98% routing accuracy after adaptation compared with 43-70% for routers trained without our approach.
