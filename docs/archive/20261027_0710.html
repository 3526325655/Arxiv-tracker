Where Rounding Happens Matters: Rounding-Location Alignment Stabilizes LLM Reinforcement Learning
Authors: Yijing Ke, Tianle Cai, Ruosong Wang
First: 2026-10-23T17:45:03+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-23T17:45:03+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Reinforcement learning for large language models often uses separate systems for inference and training. Even with identical weights and BF16 tensors, implementation differences can make an intended on-policy update off-policy and destabilize training. Existing remedies align the entire inference and training stacks, change global precision, or modify the learning algorithm. We ask whether aligning selected parts of the execution stack can stabilize BF16 RL while other implementation differences remain. We align training-side rounding locations with inference in three regions---RoPE, SwiGLU, and residual RMSNorm---by retaining selected intermediates in FP32 until their BF16 outputs. Across four Qwen-family models, aligning all three regions prevents the collapse observed under the default BF16 baseline; RoPE-path alignment alone also suffices in the studied runs, despite remaining numerical discrepancies. The interventions keep the inference engine, matrix-multiplication and attention kernels, and learning objective unchanged.

Monetizing Intelligence: Advertising and Subscription Design on LLM Platforms
Authors: Yuhang Ji, Fasheng Xu, Tianjun Feng
First: 2026-10-23T17:39:42+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-23T17:39:42+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large language model (LLM) platforms introducing advertising must decide how much capability ad revenue should fund and what consumers should pay to avoid ads. We study a monopolistic platform that jointly chooses costly response quality, advertising intensity, and subscription prices when advertising reduces user value but better service raises advertising returns. Three findings follow. First, when partly ad-supported and ad-free plans coexist, advertising can finance useful service for consumers who receive none under pure subscription. Lower effective quality in middle plans weakens the alternatives available to premium users, allowing higher prices for unchanged ad-free service. Some fully ad-supported users gain utility, while partly ad-supported and ad-free users lose: broader access can coexist with utility polarization. Second, an intermediate advertising cap can maximize consumer surplus by preserving some revenue that finances service while limiting degradation. Third, cheaper compute can raise quality and reduce ad intensity, yet sufficiently large cost reductions can increase aggregate advertising revenue above its initial level because better service raises the value of the remaining advertised interactions. An extension shows how advertising-funded upgrades can bring plans to the same maximum capability and advertising exposure, eliminating the basis for different prices. These results make menu design central to evaluating LLM advertising: the consequences extend beyond users who see ads to the alternatives and prices faced by those who do not.

Green Code or Costly Code? Evaluating the Energy Costs of Iterative LLM-Based Code Optimizations
Authors: Sadra Emami, Benjamin Weigell, Mohammad Reza Mousavi, Gunel Jahangirova, Hugo Araujo, Bernhard Bauer
First: 2026-10-23T16:58:01+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-23T16:58:01+00:00
Comments: Accepted at the 49th IEEE/ACM International Conference on Software Engineering (ICSE 2027)
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used to optimize code for performance. This practice often assumes that improvements in execution speed naturally translate into overall lower energy consumption. We challenge this assumption empirically by measuring whether performance-optimized code produced by LLM self-refinement pipelines is also energy-efficient relative to canonical human reference solutions, and whether the energy cost of the optimization process can be offset by subsequent execution-time savings. We evaluate 11 open-source models across four decoding configurations on all 1,000 problems of EffiBench, a state-of-the-art performance benchmark, while collecting per-process, hardware-level energy measurements throughout. Inference energy spans 0.79 to 8.68 MJ across model-variant pairs, which is roughly equivalent to the energy of 250 to 2800 GPT-4.1 queries. Limiting the maximum number of output tokens has the largest impact, reducing energy use by up to 69% compared to the default configuration. On the execution side, 42 of 44 configurations produce code that consumes more energy per problem than the canonical human reference, with overheads ranging from +4% to +886%. The two exceptions achieve only marginal gains, with break-even points on the order of 10^6 to 10^7 executions. Mixture-of-Experts architectures consume substantially less inference energy than equivalently-sized dense models without sacrificing correctness, making architectural choice a more consequential sustainability lever than decoding parameters. Performance optimization and energy efficiency are not always aligned goals: LLM self-refinement consistently produces faster-seeking but energy-heavier code relative to what a human engineer would write. NgramRL: Accelerating RL Post-Training for Structured Generation via Rollout-Aware N-gram Speculative Decoding
Authors: Nilesh Prasad Pandey, Jangseon Park, Jason Kong, Onat Gungor, Tajana Rosing
First: 2026-10-23T16:47:29+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-23T16:47:29+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) post-training improves the reasoning capabilities of large language models, but its cost is dominated by autoregressive rollout generation, which can exceed 70% of training time. Existing speculative-decoding methods are poorly suited to this setting: model-based drafters consume scarce GPU memory and may become misaligned as the policy evolves, while existing model-free methods do not explicitly exploit repetition across training epochs. We observe that recent rollouts of the same prompt provide a high-quality, self-updating draft source. Based on this insight, we propose NgramRL, a per-prompt n-gram drafter constructed from bounded recent rollout history. NgramRL requires no auxiliary model, drafter training, or persistent GPU-resident drafter state, while target-model verification preserves the rollout distribution. To hide CPU cache lookup and scheduling, we further introduce an adaptive fan-out prefetcher that predicts likely verification outcomes (j, b), consisting of the accepted draft length j and target-emitted continuation token b. A causal budget allocator concentrates prefetches on high-probability outcomes, while uncovered outcomes fall back to sequential lookup without changing committed tokens. Across two model families and three reasoning and structured-generation workloads, NgramRL commits 3.60-7.77 tokens per target-model forward in the warm-cache regime, up to 1.99x more than SuffixDecoding and 7.0x more than prompt-lookup decoding. On Spider, the prefetcher covers 96.6% of Qwen3-8B outcomes at a candidate budget of 16 and improves measured rollout throughput by 1.38x over synchronous NgramRL, approaching its 1.40x overlap ceiling.

Abstract
Medical deep learning models depend heavily on domain-specific knowledge to perform well on knowledge-intensive clinical tasks. Prior work has primarily leveraged unimodal knowledge graphs, such as the Unified Medical Language System (UMLS), to enhance model performance. However, integrating multimodal medical knowledge graphs remains largely underexplored, mainly due to the lack of resources linking imaging data with clinical concepts. To address this gap, we propose MEDMKG, a Medical Multimodal Knowledge Graph that unifies visual and textual medical information through a multi-stage construction pipeline. MEDMKG fuses the rich multimodal data from MIMIC-CXR with the structured clinical knowledge from UMLS, utilizing both rule-based tools and large language models for accurate concept extraction and relationship modeling. To ensure graph quality and compactness, we introduce Neighbor-aware Filtering (NaF), a novel filtering algorithm tailored for multimodal knowledge graphs. We evaluate MEDMKG across three tasks under two experimental settings, benchmarking twenty-four baseline methods and four state-of-the-art vision-language backbones on six datasets. Results show that MEDMKG not only improves performance in downstream medical tasks but also offers a strong foundation for developing adaptive and robust strategies for multimodal knowledge integration in medical artificial intelligence.

Trusting the Tool: Asymmetric Deference of LLM Vulnerability Auditors to Static-Analysis Evidence
Authors: ZhiHong Liu, Shuyao Gao
First: 2026-10-23T16:45:10+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-23T16:45:10+00:00
Comments: 33 pages, 9 figures, 5 tables. Submitted to IEEE Access. Code: https://github.com/lzh0082-cloud/CODE-DETECTION
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Pipelines that combine large language models (LLMs) with static analysis let the model consume the analyser's output as evidence, but whether the model distinguishes true from false evidence, and whether its scrutiny depends on what the evidence claims, has not been measured. We built an intraprocedural analyser for C/C++ that produces proof obligations and audited vulnerable and patched functions from PrimeVul under six evidence conditions that differ only in the notes, including fabricated assurances that a missing check exists and fabricated alarms that an added check is missing. The study covers 916 pairs in four disjoint sets, eleven model configurations from four vendors and 16,572 audits; no model is trained or fine-tuned. True evidence barely improved accuracy (+0.02). False evidence acted asymmetrically: relative to true notes about the same operations, false assurances lowered the detection of real vulnerabilities by up to 19 points, whereas false alarms never significantly lowered accuracy and were disputed far more often. An internally registered replication on seven new models confirmed that false assurances move verdicts towards their claim more than false alarms do (mean asymmetry +0.16, 95% CI +0.10 to +0.24). Instructions in the audit prompt did not reliably reduce deference; the best one failed to replicate. Verifying each claim in a separate call and showing the refuted claims raised accuracy under false assurances by 12 points in a pre-specified test (p=0.002) without loss under true evidence; for a second model the gain was smaller and not significant. Pipelines should therefore verify a tool's guarantees before the model relies on them, and studies should measure deference in a direction-aware way.

How Do I Know What to Say Next? Barenholtz's Autogenerative Theory as an Enrichment of Harrisean Integrationism
Authors: J. Mark Bishop, Stephen J. Cowley
First: 2026-07-08T19:54:18+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-23T16:15:31+00:00
Comments: Submitted to Philosophy and Technology
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Roy Harris's Integrationist linguistics offers a compelling critique of the referentialist tradition embedded deep at the heart of computational approaches to language, arguing that language is not a code that maps onto a pre-given world but a situated, bipartite activity oriented toward prospective joint action. Yet Integrationism leaves certain explanatory gaps: it does not fully account for the structural mechanism by which signs sustain prospective openness, it undertheorises the continuity between linguistic and non-linguistic semiotic activity, and it offers no detailed account of the structural properties of the accumulated archive of past integrations. This paper argues that Elan Barenholtz's autogenerative theory of language, developed in response to the behaviour of Large Language Models (LLMs), can fill precisely these gaps, enriching Integrationism without undermining any of its core commitments. Specifically, the autogenerative account provides: a structural mechanism for the prospective openness that Harris identifies as central to bipartite communication; a computational correlate for Harris's thesis of semiotic continuity between language and other sign-making activity; and a theory of the archive: what the accumulated residue of past integrations looks like and how new participants draw upon it. The synthesis preserves Harris's ontological primacy of the situated integrative act while adding explanatory content that Integrationism itself does not supply. For practitioners and researchers in natural language processing and large language model design, the argument offers a principled account of what the statistical structure that LLMs so effectively exploit actually is, and of what it cannot, by its nature, provide.

Architecture-Aware Reinforcement Learning Makes Sliding-Window Attention Competitive in Math Reasoning
Authors: Kai Liu, Peijie Dong, Xinchen Xie, Jianfei Gao, Qipeng Guo, Xiaowen Chu, Shaoting Zhang, Kai Chen
First: 2026-06-10T03:56:03+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-23T15:47:04+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
The rapid progress of reasoning and agentic large language models (LLMs) has increased the demand for long-context inference, but self-attention (SA) scales quadratically with context length. To address this, we study SWARR (Sliding-Window Attention with Reinforced Adaptation for Math Reasoning), a practical recipe for adapting SWA models to mathematical reasoning. SWARR has two stages: (1) efficient conversion from a pretrained SA model to SWA with supervised fine-tuning (SFT), which avoids pretraining a new base model, and (2) policy adaptation with reinforcement learning (RL). We find that SWA still underperforms SA after SFT, and we hypothesize that this gap is caused in part by a data-architecture mismatch: most SFT data are prepared for SA models and may contain long-range dependencies that are difficult for SWA to model. Because on-policy RL optimizes self-generated trajectories under the SWA constraint, it can adapt trajectories to better match SWA. Experiments on mathematical reasoning benchmarks show that this recipe substantially narrows the gap between SWA and SA, recovering much of the accuracy lost during SWA conversion while preserving the efficiency benefits of linear-complexity attention. Our central contribution is the empirical finding that RL changes the conclusion one would draw from conversion and SFT alone about SWA's viability for math reasoning.

Abstract
The rise of Artificial Intelligence and Large Language Models is driving increased GPU usage in data centers for complex training and inference tasks, impacting operational costs, energy demands, and the environmental footprint of large-scale computing infrastructures. This work addresses the online scheduling problem in GPU datacenters, which involves scheduling tasks without knowledge of their future arrivals. We focus on two objectives: minimizing GPU fragmentation and reducing power consumption. GPU fragmentation occurs when partial GPU allocations hinder the efficient use of remaining resources, especially as the datacenter nears full capacity. Generalization properties of the token perceptron: the role of embedding and vocabulary
Authors: Dario Bocchi, Matteo Negri, Flavio Nicoletti, Francesco D'Amico
First: 2026-10-23T15:35:19+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-23T15:35:19+00:00
Comments: 39 pages, 9 figures
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large language models are trained to predict tokens from a finite vocabulary, each represented by a vector in a $d$-dimensional embedding space. Therefore, token prediction is a classification problem whose classes have a geometry determined by the embedding. We study how this geometry affects learning in a minimal solvable model: a linear predictor that maps a sequence of $N$ embedded tokens to a $d$-dimensional output through one $d\times d$ matrix per position, trained in the teacher--student setting. We call this the \textit{token perceptron}. We compute its generalization error with the replica method, taking $N\to\infty$ at fixed number of examples per position $α=P/N$ and keeping $d$ and the vocabulary size $D$ finite. As a first reference case, we consider continuous embeddings on the sphere, which isolate the role of the embedding dimension. The natural load is then $α/d$, and for $d>1$ a normalized label fixes the direction of the teacher output, so the problem becomes a noiseless regression: the teacher is recovered exactly above $α_{\rm rec}=d^2/(d-1)$, and spherical cross-entropy reaches this threshold at large inverse temperature. In the same setting the storage capacity of random associations scales as $d^2$ for margins $O(1/\sqrt{d})$. We then turn to vocabulary embeddings, where a label only identifies the Voronoi cell of the teacher output, so that each example imposes inequalities rather than equations, as in the classical perceptron. We compute the Bayes-optimal error for a given embedding matrix through an approximate spectral closure and show that the optimal learner first resolves the directions in which the vocabulary has the largest variance, even when the teacher is isotropic.
Summary / 总结
Large language models are trained to predict tokens from a finite vocabulary, each represented by a vector in a $d$-dimensional embedding space.

Abstract
The rapid growth of video data has created an urgent need for efficient video understanding systems that can handle long and continuous visual inputs, posing significant challenges for Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) due to severe memory bottlenecks. Moreover, existing compression methods often overlook the explosive accumulation of approximation errors. To address this issue, we propose Video-MemComp, a training-free and sensitivity-aware context management framework for memory-constrained streaming video understanding. Our method theoretically decomposes compression error into orthogonal local approximation error and layer-wise structural sensitivity via a first-order Taylor expansion. To minimize local approximation error, we perform token aggregation in a pre-RoPE safe zone with an attention-conserving logit bias, mitigating geometric distortions caused by RoPE rotations. To manage layer-wise structural sensitivity, we dynamically allocate the KV cache budget based on offline gradient priors and online context entropy, prioritizing high-sensitivity layers. Video-MemComp outperforms or achieves performance comparable to full-cache and state-of-the-art compression methods on benchmarks including MLVU, VideoMME, and EgoSchema. Notably, it improves the overall accuracy of Qwen2.5-VL-3B from 62.3% to 64.1% while reducing the KV cache to an equivalent of 6,000 tokens per layer, saving over 90% of KV memory.

Abstract
AI representation is emerging in digital democracy, assisting in voter emulation and decision-making. Large language models (LLMs) behave differently depending on the model, the prompt context, and the voter information they receive, representing some groups more accurately than others and potentially making the emulation unfair to certain groups. We investigate demographic biases in LLM-emulated electorates: how accurately LLMs represent individual votes, whether some voter groups are represented more accurately than others, and whether AI alignment can reduce these disparities without sacrificing overall accuracy. Common alignment strategies, such as repeated queries or fairness instructions, do not reduce these errors and can double them.

Abstract
With the rapid progress of large language models (LLMs), an interesting question has emerged: how do LLM-based agents perform in Minecraft? To date, most Minecraft benchmarks evaluate them under fixed game mechanics, which are extensively documented online. High performance in these settings does not show whether an agent can continue making progress when familiar recipes, drops, and other rules change. In this paper, we introduce MirrorCraft, a paired benchmark for evaluating agents under hidden rule changes in Minecraft. Each Mirror world is a copy of its paired Vanilla world, with selected server-side rules silently modified by the datapack of one rule suite. Terrain, spawn, resource placement, task, interface, and action budget remain matched within every Vanilla-Mirror pair. MirrorCraft includes 10 Vanilla worlds in five controlled biomes, six rule suites, and three progression tasks, and we evaluate two LLMs and six agent configurations under a shared Mineflayer interface. We measure progress with server-verified advancement milestones and completion with success rate, and define the Rule Intervention Effect (RIE) as the performance change between paired Vanilla and Mirror worlds. Experiments with 8,640 episodes show that hidden rule changes have sharply different effects across suites. Among the configurations evaluated without rule descriptions, ReAct, Reflexion, and Voyager achieve the highest pooled Mirror Scores, but ReAct loses the most under compressed resource drops. Providing ReAct with the exact rules yields modest gains in average progress and completion across all three tasks, yet most of the difference between suites remains once the rules are known. MirrorCraft extends Minecraft evaluation beyond fixed mechanics and provides a controlled setting for studying how agents use gameplay outcomes when the rules of the current world differ from familiar ones.

Abstract
On-policy self-distillation enables post-training foundation models without relying on stronger external teachers. However, existing approaches rely on ground-truth question-answer pairs, human-curated problem sets, or other supervised signals. In this work, we reformulate on-policy self-distillation as a self-training framework, driven by self-generated question-answer pairs and privileged reasoning demonstrations. Same Answer, Different Confidence: Protocol Sensitivity in LLM Confidence Calibration
Authors: Hankyeol Kim, Pilsung Kang
First: 2026-05-26T23:03:38+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-23T14:58:08+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large language models (LLM) can state their confidence in an answer (verbalized confidence), or the confidence can be read from the probability of the answer's tokens (token confidence). Comparisons of the two ask which is better calibrated, but the token probability of an answer depends on the text that precedes it. Across five open LLMs and four question-answering datasets, we fix each answer, its verbalized confidence, and its correctness label, and vary how each score is measured. The scores themselves change with the protocol, and so does the comparison between them. Changing the context in which the token probability is read shifts the gap between the two scores by about the size of the gap itself (.287 vs. .304 in expected calibration error, ECE; .189 vs. .145 in AUROC). The 95% interval of the shift excludes zero in 19 and 17 of the 20 settings, and under AUROC the shift has the same sign in all of them, so the context changes how answers are ranked. The better score flips in 8 settings under each metric, counting only flips that the intervals support, and the shift also appears in four larger models of up to 72B parameters. Most of the shift appears with changes to the instruction, not to the position of the answer. Other measurement choices also matter, and stated confidence depends on how a supplied answer is written. We turn these results into a reporting checklist for LLM confidence and its comparisons.

Do Language Models Trust Their Own Justifications? A Study on Functional Consistency
Authors: Alisson Rosa Pereira, Bruno Iochins Grisci
Venue: 2026 International Joint Conference on Neural Networks (IJCNN), Maastricht, Netherlands, 2026, pp. 1-6

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have been widely adopted in text classification tasks, where they not only output class predictions but also generate explanations that highlight the tokens deemed most relevant for reaching the predicted label. Yet it remains unclear whether these highlighted elements are behaviorally consistent with the model's own predictions under targeted interventions, which we refer to as functional auto-consistency. While much of the literature evaluates the textual plausibility of such explanations, few studies assess their functional consistency with the model's actual behavior. In this work, we propose an experimental framework based on the principle of auto-consistency: if a model identifies certain tokens as decisive, then isolating, removing, or semantically inverting them should produce systematic and interpretable changes in its predictions. We operationalize this evaluation through sufficiency, comprehensiveness, and counterfactuality metrics, and conduct experiments on IMDB and Steam reviews across both closed-source (GPT-4o-mini) and open-source LLMs (Gemma3, Granite8B, DeepSeek). Results show that GPT-4o-mini follows the expected progression across all metrics, Gemma3 and Granite8B maintain coherence under sufficiency but lose consistency under more demanding interventions, while DeepSeek variants display structural deviations, either failing to preserve sufficiency or overreacting under comprehensiveness and counterfactuality. These findings show that explanation reliability varies across LLM families and scales, with smaller models displaying contradictions and larger ones exhibiting over-sensitivity. By combining sufficiency, comprehensiveness, and counterfactuality, our approach provides a systematic methodology for assessing the functional consistency of LLM self-explanations.

Abstract
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) read images through visual tokens, and under the small per-image budgets that efficient deployment requires, the fine text of documents, charts and infographics becomes illegible. Zooming in recovers the evidence, but the region must be chosen before the model answers. We find that MLLMs know where the evidence is, but not in a form that can be read before they answer: the answer tokens localize it from the middle layers on, whereas the attention available before answering does so only in the top layers, at a depth that varies across models, and diffusely. Supervising the pre-answer attention to point degrades reading, and the benefit of zooming is governed by each image's information gap. Building on these findings, we introduce ours, which lets an MLLM look where its answer would. A dedicated look token, processed in a single prefill and kept out of the answering pass, learns in hindsight to attend as the model's own correct answers do, extracting this knowledge into a concentrated pointer before any answer exists. Its hidden state then selects how to look, from no zoom to crops, square windows, windows that adapt to the pointer and foveated warps, supervised by the counterfactual outcome of every view. ours needs no box annotations, per-model layer search, external localizers or generation before the answer. On Qwen3-VL at three scales and five text-rich benchmarks, it surpasses the strongest baseline by 4.8-9.1 points at 98 visual tokens per view and attention-guided zooming by 11.1-17.0 points, while running faster than attention-guided and grounding-based zooming.

Abstract
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) often know the rule but pick the wrong answer: on abstract visual reasoning (AVR) tasks, a model can describe what it sees and name the underlying pattern, yet still fail to choose the matching candidate. Existing AVR benchmarks cannot detect this because they collapse perception, rule induction, and answer selection into a single right-or-wrong signal. We introduce StemBind, a shared-stem diagnostic benchmark that probes the same visual stem with three aligned questions: Perception (what is in the image), Rule (what pattern governs it), and Full (which option completes it), so a final-answer error can be attributed to a specific sub-step on the same evidence. StemBind contains 2,298 curated knowledge-light stems across nine auditable visual operations, totaling 19,533 P/R/F tasks, with each full item annotated by Sternberg's four reasoning stages (S1 Encode, S2 Infer, S3 Map, S4 Apply). Evaluating 24 frontier MLLM configurations yields four findings. (i) The R-F chasm: rule accuracy exceeds full-item accuracy on 22 of 24 models, so most failures happen after the rule is identified. (ii) A persistent binding gap: even when P and R are both correct on the same stem, models still answer F incorrectly 51.2% of the time. (iii) The bottleneck is S3: process diagnostics and Stage-wise Stimulus Augmentation localize the dominant failure to rule-to-instance mapping. (iv) Scaling and thinking do not help: neither larger models nor explicit thinking mode reliably closes the gap, and thinking even lowers rule and full-item accuracy. StemBind reframes AVR evaluation from final-answer ranking to locating where abstract visual reasoning breaks down, identifying rule-to-instance binding as a concrete next target for vision-grounded reasoning.

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) increasingly serve as reasoning components in modern software systems, yet a model that answers a logical query correctly can contradict itself when the same problem is restated in an equivalent form. Static benchmarks evaluate queries in isolation and therefore fail to expose such inconsistencies. To address this limitation, we present LGMT, an open-source tool that detects reasoning inconsistencies through metamorphic relations grounded in first-order logic. Abstract

Abstract
Continuous diffusion models have emerged as a promising non-autoregressive paradigm for text generation over continuous embedding spaces. Along the continuous trajectory, the residual token identity directly shapes the denoising objective. However, the geometric trajectory misaligns with temporal progression, triggering severe temporal compression. In this paper, we show that embedding norm decay causes Euclidean distance to yield spurious overtaking, indicating that token competition must be evaluated directionally. In this space, a candidate overtakes the source token only when the effective noise magnitude surpasses their semantic separation. We formalize these pairwise dynamics as the Competition Threshold Theory (CTT). Crucially, high-dimensional overtaking requires extreme noise levels, causing standard trajectories to concentrate identity decay into a narrow window near the noisy endpoint. To resolve this, we propose Semantic Retention Scheduling (SRS) to align time allocation with the progression of identity decay, and demonstrate that existing token identity metrics are fundamentally statistical aggregations of these pairwise crossings. Building on these theoretical insights, we introduce LLaCO, a generative framework powered by competition-aware semantic scheduling. LLaCO adapts a pretrained backbone to continuous representations via an embedding directional adapter and timestep-conditioned modulation. To operate within the directional space, LLaCO employs a spherical noising process formulated as a geodesic interpolation toward a mask-biased endpoint, eliminating norm decay while preserving accurate token competition. Guided by SRS, LLaCO reallocates the denoising trajectory proportionally to the logarithmic rank decay. Instantiated as an 8B model, LLaCO outperforms previous continuous diffusion baselines and rivals discrete diffusion models of comparable scale.

Abstract
Balancing exploration with exploitation is a core challenge in black-box optimization and reinforcement learning. We introduce POETS ($\textbf{Po}$licy $\textbf{E}$nsembles for $\textbf{T}$hompson $\textbf{S}$ampling), a novel framework that bridges uncertainty quantification and policy optimization. Our approach is grounded in the insight that policies trained with Kullback-Leibler (KL) regularization implicitly encode an underlying reward function. Abstract
Cinematic video generation requires both temporal inference from limited inputs (i.e., sparse rather than frame-by-frame guidance) and factor-aware control of character, scene, action, and camera. Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) offer useful priors for both capabilities, but their representations are not automatically actionable for video generation: a Video Diffusion Transformer (DiT) may bypass them through native conditioning pathways, and using them does not ensure either factor-aware control or generation-compatible temporal prediction. First, latent-only generator grounding trains the DiT with a frozen MLLM and connector, strengthening the DiT's use of MLLM latents for generation. Second, for factor-aware control, paired videos specify a target-factor change and non-target preservation, and factor-span replacement trains the DiT to realize this relationship in its output. Third, for temporal inference, text-only and text-plus-complete-video observations share a video target, and the trained DiT is then frozen while the MLLM and connector are optimized through it. This final stage trains limited-input representations for generation while retaining complete-observation reconstruction as a complementary training mode. Extensive experiments demonstrate our consistently better performance on three cinematic video generation tasks (i.e., sparse-to-video, factor-aware editing, and multi-shot generation) over baselines and validate FAPL's contributions to MLLM feature use, temporal control, and factor coordination.

Abstract
